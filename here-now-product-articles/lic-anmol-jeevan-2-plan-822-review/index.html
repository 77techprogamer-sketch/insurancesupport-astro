LIC Anmol Jeevan 2 (Plan 822) Review 2026: Pure Term Rates, Revival, Live Policy Value

₹6–24 lakh of pure term cover, withdrawn in 2020, still in force for thousands — and a revival window half as long as every other LIC plan.

LIC's Anmol Jeevan II (Plan 822, UIN 512N285V01) is the plainest life cover LIC has ever issued: pay premiums during the term, and if the life assured dies, the nominee gets the sum assured — nothing else happens, ever. Launched in 2014 and withdrawn from new business on 1 February 2020, it sat at the bottom of LIC's protection ladder for six years, and a very large block of ₹6–24 lakh policies is still in force today. This review covers what the plan actually promised (the full benefit and eligibility tables from LIC's own sales literature), what a live policy is worth versus surrendering it, the two-year revival quirk that separates it from every other LIC plan, what happened when the insurer retired it, and where plain-term buyers go now.

The ₹6–24 lakh band is the defining constraint: it brackets the plan between LIC's entry buyers and everyone needing serious cover. Above ₹25 lakh, the same brochure generation pointed buyers to Amulya Jeevan II (Plan 823) — same pure-term mechanics, sum assured ₹25 lakh and above, term 5 to 35 years. Between them the two plans covered ₹6 lakh to the top of the market with zero sophistication, which was exactly the point.

Revival: the two-year window that catches people

Most LIC plans revive within five consecutive years from the first unpaid premium. Anmol Jeevan II's brochure says something stricter: revival within two consecutive years from the date of first unpaid premium, but before policy term expiry, by paying all arrears with interest compounded half-yearly at the rate fixed by LIC at the time of payment — subject to satisfactory evidence of continued insurability.

The two-year clock — not the usual five — is plan 822 specific; miss it and surrender is moot because surrender never existed. Only death cover during the term remains the contract.

Two consequences follow. First, lapsed 822 policyholders have roughly half the usual runway to act. Second, because the plan has no surrender value, a lapsed policy outside its revival window is worth exactly zero — there is no residual fund to salvage, so the revival decision is binary and urgent. Nominees' claims on lapsed policies are decided under the standard suicide and reinstatement clauses; the revival letter is the document that ends the argument.

Should a live 822 policy be kept?

Almost always yes, and the reason is the premium, not loyalty. A policy issued to a 25-year-old in 2016 locks in mid-twenties term rates for a 20–25 year term. Buying the same ₹20 lakh of cover today at age 35 costs roughly the age-30-to-40 band of the table above — materially more, for a shorter remaining horizon. The policy cannot be "improved" into anything, but it can be supplemented: riders were not part of 822's design, so additional cover is bought as separate policies — another term plan for the gap, and our family protection planning guide frames how much is actually enough.

What live policyholders keep

  • Level sum assured ₹6–24 lakh, payable on death during term — no conditions beyond premium payment and the standard clauses
  • Original-issue premium rates, typically far below today's quotes for the same age band
  • Section 80C deduction on premiums; death amount tax-free under 10(10D)
  • Simple, provable claim path — one benefit, one document set

What it can never do

  • No maturity or surrender value — every rupee of premium buys protection only
  • No riders inside the plan; disability/accident extras need separate policies
  • Maximum ₹24 lakh — often below a modern family's actual exposure
  • Two-year revival window punishes lapses harder than any other LIC plan

After the withdrawal: what replaced it

LIC withdrew Anmol Jeevan II from new business on 1 February 2020. Existing policies continue unchanged — withdrawal stops issuance, not contracts. In the current line-up, plain-term protection for the mass band is served by newer term tables with wider sum-assured ranges and optional riders, while the ₹25 lakh-plus tier remains Amulya Jeevan II's territory. The structural lesson from 822's retirement is worth more than the plan itself: LIC's protection catalogue is actively curated, and every retirement is a signal that the insurer's own pricing or risk view moved. How 822 sits against today's term market

Withdrawn term plans are judged by two questions, and they have different answers. Is the cover still adequate? Often not on its own: ₹24 lakh was respectable purchasing power in 2014, and a decade of inflation plus a home loan and two school fees later, most families find the actual replacement need measured in crores, not lakhs. Is the premium still competitive? Usually yes, dramatically: a 2016-vintage 822 policy can cost a third of what a 36-year-old is quoted today for the same ₹20 lakh. The correct response to both answers is the same — keep the legacy policy running because its price is unbeatable, and buy a separate modern term policy sized to today's exposure rather than trying to "replace" the old one. Layering two term policies also splits underwriting risk: if a health development would complicate a fresh large-policy application, the small legacy cover still stands untouched. Sizing logic — income multiples, loan outstanding, education provision — belongs in a protection plan, and our Insurance Support planning desk works it policy by policy.

Claiming on an 822 policy

Term claims are documents, not arguments. The nominee needs the claim form, the policy bond, the death certificate, identity and relationship proof, and the bank details for the payout — medical or police documentation only where the circumstances invite it (accidental death, death within the early years). Two clauses govern the edge cases: death by suicide within the first year of the policy (or of revival) is settled under the statutory 80% refund-of-premium terms rather than the sum assured, and any premium dispute is best resolved before filing rather than during. Is LIC Anmol Jeevan II (Plan 822) still available?

No — it was withdrawn from new business on 1 February 2020. Existing policies remain fully valid: premiums continue, the ₹6–24 lakh death benefit stands for the policy term, and claims are settled on the original wordings. Withdrawal only means LIC no longer issues new policies under this table.

Does Anmol Jeevan II pay anything on maturity or surrender?

Nothing. It is a pure term plan: on survival to the end of the term no amount is payable, and the plan carries no surrender value or loan facility at any point. Every rupee of premium buys death cover during the term — which is also why the premiums are so low for the cover provided.

My 822 policy lapsed five years ago. Can I revive it?

No. Anmol Jeevan II's revival window is two consecutive years from the date of the first unpaid premium (compared with the usual five on most LIC plans), subject to arrears plus half-yearly-compounded interest and evidence of continued insurability. Outside that window the policy cannot be revived, and since there is no surrender value, it carries no residual worth.

Anmol Jeevan II or Amulya Jeevan II — which band applies?

Anmol Jeevan II covered ₹6 lakh to ₹24 lakh with terms of 5–25 years; Amulya Jeevan II (Plan 823) is the same pure-term design for sum assured of ₹25 lakh and above with terms up to 35 years. The split is purely by cover size — both pay only the sum assured on death, with no maturity benefit.

How is a death claim on a live 822 policy paid?

The nominee files the standard claim set — claim form, policy bond, death certificate, nominee identity and relationship proof, and bank account details for the payout. Death by suicide within one year of issue or revival settles under the statutory refund-of-premium terms instead of the sum assured. Is ₹20–24 lakh of term cover enough in 2026?

Rarely on its own. A working rule many planners use is 10–15 times annual income, adjusted for loan outstanding, children's education costs and the spouse's income gap — in an Indian metro that arithmetic usually lands in crores for a primary earner. Anmol Jeevan II's band caps at ₹24 lakh, so a live policy is best treated as the cheap base layer, with a modern term policy sized to today's need bought alongside it.
